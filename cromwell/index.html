Oliver Cromwell

A Huntingdonshire farmer who became Parliament's best general, signed the king's death warrant, conquered Ireland and ruled all three kingdoms as Lord Protector. Prelude

How it came to this

Three kingdoms, three different churches and one king. The pressures that exploded in the 1640s had been building for a century.

  1. 1536 to 1603Conquest and Reformation

    The Tudors reconquer Ireland, but the Reformation leaves Ireland mostly Catholic while England and Scotland become Protestant.

  2. 1603Three kingdoms, one king

    James VI of Scotland inherits England and Ireland. Three kingdoms with different churches now share a Stuart king.

  3. 1609The Plantation of Ulster

    Land taken from Gaelic lords who fled in 1607 is settled with English and Scottish Protestants, planting a grievance that erupts in 1641.

  4. 1625Charles I

    The new king marries a French Catholic princess, Henrietta Maria. Puritans fear popery at the heart of the court.

  5. 1629 to 1640Rule without Parliament

    Charles governs alone, raising money without Parliament and backing church changes that Puritans see as Catholic in all but name.

  6. 1637 to 1640The Scots revolt

    A new prayer book sparks rebellion in Scotland. To pay for war Charles must recall Parliament, and the crisis that makes Cromwell begins.

    Epilogue

    What came after

    The republic died within two years of Cromwell, but the land settlement and the union he forced outlived him by centuries.

    1. 1660 to 1662The king returns

      Monarchy, Lords and bishops are restored. Some Catholic landowners recover estates, but most Cromwellian grants in Ireland stand.

    2. 1688 to 1691A second revolution

      James II is replaced by William and Mary. William's victory at the Boyne in 1690 and the fall of Limerick bring a new round of Irish confiscations.

    3. 1695 to 1728The Penal Laws

      Catholics are barred from buying land, holding office, voting and much else. By the 1770s they own around 5% of Irish land.

    4. 1707 and 1801Union again

      Scotland, then Ireland, joins England in one parliament, as Cromwell's Commonwealth had briefly done in the 1650s.

    5. 1829 to 1909Emancipation and land

      Catholic Emancipation, then a series of Land Acts, let Irish tenant farmers buy back the land, undoing much of the Cromwellian settlement.

    6. 1922Independence

      The Irish Free State is founded, while Northern Ireland stays in the United Kingdom. Cromwell remains a byword in Irish memory.

    Two islands, two memories

    In Britain he is argued over. In Ireland he is remembered for one thing above all. The figures below show why the difference runs so deep.

    In Britain

    • 1845Thomas Carlyle's edition of his letters turns him from usurper into Victorian hero, taken up by Nonconformists and Liberals.
    • 1899His bronze statue by Hamo Thornycroft goes up outside the Houses of Parliament, where it still stands.
    • 2002Voted 10th in the BBC's 100 Greatest Britons, ahead of every monarch except Elizabeth I.
    • 2004A group of MPs, including Tony Banks, tables a motion to have the statue melted down. It goes nowhere.
    • TodayClaimed at different times by radicals, Liberals and parliamentarians, and still pictured as the Puritan who cancelled Christmas. The Cromwell Museum in his old Huntingdon school and his house at Ely keep his story local.

    In Ireland

    • 1650s‘Mallacht Chromail ort’, the curse of Cromwell on you, enters the language as one of the worst things you can wish on someone.
    • 1650s‘To Hell or to Connacht’ becomes the folk summary of the transplantation, though there is no record of him saying it.
    • 1895Irish nationalist MPs, joined by Conservatives, defeat public funding for the Westminster statue. Willie Redmond calls him ‘a canting, hypocritical murderer’.
    • 2000Drogheda historian Tom Reilly displays Cromwell's death mask in the town and is challenged to a duel by the deputy mayor.
    • TodayDrogheda and Wexford are taught as massacres, and his name stands for conquest and dispossession across the island.
    The Down Survey

    Mapping a conquest

    Between 1654 and 1656 about a thousand of Cromwell's soldiers walked Ireland with measuring chains, so that conquered land could be handed out to pay the army and Parliament's investors. Drag the slider to see how the land changed hands, county by county, then tap a county for its story.

    How the survey worked

    • Fast. William Petty, an army doctor, signed the contract on 24 December 1654 and promised the job in thirteen months. The survey was accepted in May 1656.
    • Soldiers, not surveyors. About a thousand men, mostly unemployed soldiers, measured with chains using simple instruments Petty designed. Draughtsmen in Dublin plotted their figures onto gridded paper.
    • Profitable or not. Each parcel was marked as arable, meadow, pasture, wood, bog or mountain. Grants were reckoned in profitable acres, so bog and mountain often came along uncounted.
    • Close, not exact. Checked against modern measurement, its acreages run on average just over 11.5% short.
    • Huge. Petty counted 2,278 parishes. About 1,400 parish maps were drawn, each with a written terrier listing owners, religion and land use.

    Measure like Petty

    The survey used the Irish plantation perch of 21 feet, not the English perch of 16.5 feet. With 160 square perches to the acre either way, an Irish acre is about 1.62 English acres.

    The maps' afterlife

    • 1656The survey is accepted and used to hand out land to soldiers and investors.
    • 1707Petty's barony maps, sent by sea to London, are captured by French privateers. They are still in Paris, at the Bibliothèque nationale.
    • 1711Fire at the Surveyor General's office in Dublin destroys many original parish maps.
    • 1786Daniel O'Brien copies the surviving maps. His copies disappear until 1965.
    • 1908The Ordnance Survey publishes a facsimile of the Paris barony maps.
    • 1922The last original maps are destroyed in Dublin during the fighting of 1922.
    • 2013Trinity College Dublin reunites the surviving copies online, linked to a database of landowners in 1641 and 1670.

    How to read the map: each county is coloured by its main role at each stage, drawn from the Act of Satisfaction (1653) and standard accounts of the plantations.       Your verdict

      HeroVillain

      Where most historians now agree

      • He was an outstanding cavalry commander and organiser, with no military experience before the age of 43.
      • His faith was sincere. He read each victory as a sign of God's favour, and that certainty fed his ruthlessness.
      • Killing whole garrisons at Drogheda and Wexford was severe even by the harsh rules of the time, and civilians and priests died too.
      • The land confiscations, mostly carried out after he left Ireland but under his government, did more lasting damage than the sieges.
      • He protected a wider range of Protestant believers than almost any ruler of his age, while banning public Catholic worship.

      Still argued over

      • How many civilians died at Drogheda and Wexford, and whether he meant them to.
      • Whether his violence in Ireland was driven mainly by hatred of Catholics and the Irish, by revenge for 1641, or by the need to defeat royalists fast.
      • Whether words like genocide or ethnic cleansing fit the conquest and the land settlement.
      • Whether he was a sincere servant of his conscience, as Morrill argues, or a devious and ambitious politician, as Hutton argues.
      • Whether he defended Parliament or wrecked it, since he purged or dismissed one parliament after another.
